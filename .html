The integration test: If someone who had never studied mythology heard your talk, would they understand why the myth matters to your story — or would they just hear a name they don't recognize? If they'd just hear a name: you have more work to do.

Timing guide

1 · The Hook0:00 – 1:30What moment drops us into your experience?
2 · The Turn1:30 – 2:30What does a stranger need to know about myth #1?
3 · Second Myth2:30 – 4:00What does myth #2 add that myth #1 can't?
4 · The Ordeal ★4:00 – 6:30Where do myth and life fuse most completely? (Longest section)
5 · The Insight6:30 – 8:30What does the audience leave knowing?
6 · The Return8:30 – 10:00How does the opening look different now?

Total: 7–10 minutes. Under 7 = underdeveloped. Over 10 = you haven't edited. Time yourself in rehearsal. The Ordeal must be your longest beat.

Speaker's debrief — complete within 24 hours of your talk

This is not a summary of your talk. It is a window into your thinking as a speaker and mythologist. Write it seriously. Answer all four prompts.

Prompt 1

What personal experience did you choose to mythologize, and why did it feel worth saying out loud in front of people? What was hard about making it public?

Prompt 2

Which two mythologies did you draw from, and how did each one appear in your talk? Be specific — name the figure, motif, or story and explain the connection you made.

Prompt 3

Describe your visual element and explain the choices behind it. What did it add to the talk that words alone couldn't?

Prompt 4

Name one specific technique from a recommended talk that you tried to use — or wish you had used — in your own talk. Name the talk, the technique, and your connection to it.

You've studied myth. Now stand up and live it — out loud, in front of people, with your own voice.
